At some point in the history of humankind there were two open questions: How do you determine the slope of a curve at a single, exact point? And how do you measure the area of a shape with a curved boundary?
The first question was asked by the Greeks.
They were studying lines that only touch a circle once, most commonly known as the tangent line to a circle.
However, for other kinds of graphs it was a mystery, like for a straight line, a parabola, or an ellipse.
After some centuries, the answer to this question led us to the development of the derivative.
On the other hand, the second question led to the creation of the integral.
In a more specific tale, Archimedes was a master of computing areas under parabolas; however, his method was a headache.
Some years later, astronomers like Kepler needed to calculate areas of planetary orbits, and this was solved by the definition of an integral.

In general terms, the derivative allows us to give a quantitative answer to questions about the rate of change at an instant, like ``How fast is it changing right now''?
The integral allows us to give a quantitative answer to questions about the accumulation of change, like ``How much has accumulated in total over time''?
These two questions are fundamentally linked. 
If you have a graph of speed over time, the derivative gives the acceleration at any moment, and the integral gives the total distance traveled.

In this course, we only are going to focus on the first question, ``\textit{How do you determine the slope of a curve at a single, exact point?}''
The answer of this question is going to lead us to the definition of the derivative and that concept it is usefull at GPS navigation, medicine dosing, video games, econocmis, climate models, and other disciplines.

Hence, some of the applications that we are going to explore at the end of the course are related speed, growth, decay, accumulation or in a more abstract concept, flux. 

\paragraph{The big picture}
Calculus is related with arithmetic in the sense that the addition operation finds the total and the substraction operation finds a difference.
In calculus, the integration operation finds a total, meanwhile the derivative finds a rate of change.
The reason of why calculus is more related with arithmetic is because arithmetic give us tools to quantify \textbf{static} amounts, meanwhile calculus give us tools to quantify \textbf{dynamic}, changing amounts.

This is quite different from the math that you learn from previous courses.
In math 3 and math 4 it was more focused on algebra.
And algebra is more like an architect who designs the blueprint, that is the function. 
Calculus is the surveyor and engineer who uses specialized tool to take precises measurements from that blue print.

For example, algebra give us a function that models the height $h(t) = -5t^2 + 20 t +1$.
And can also allow us to answer the question \emph{When does the ball hit the ground?} by solving $h(t) = 0$.
Calculus is going to help us to answer questions like \emph{What is the ball's velocity at the exact instant?} with the derivative and \emph{What is the total distance traveled by the ball between and interval of time?} with integration.

As a wrap up, arithmetic allow us to handle numbers.
Algebra is to handle unknowns and relationships.
And now, calculus will help us to handle change itself.
Finally, the main association that I want you to keep in mind is the following:
Derivatives answers \emph{How fast right now} and the integrals answers \textit{How much in total}.
On the other hand, I also want to make an spoiler.
The integral and derivative are two sides of the same coin or also known as inverse operations.

\subsection{Interpretation of math}

Let's assume that we can quantify the relation between money that is needed to print a book with a function $f(x)$.
And we can quantify the relation between the money that we earn by selling a book $g(x)$.
We can create an equation that allows us to find the money that is equal to selling and printing books.

Now, let's imagine that the following algebraic expression represents the situation above,
\begin{gather*}
    \frac{2x}{x+7}=\frac{2x-7}{x}.
\end{gather*}
Imagine that the term $\frac{2x}{x+7}$ quantifies the cost of printing a book and the term $\frac{2x-7}{x}$ quantifies the amount of money that is earned by selling books.
We are going to find the value of $x$ that satisfies this condition.

In order to find this condition, we can manipulate the expression using algebraic properties to the following,
\begin{gather*}
    7x-49 = 0.
\end{gather*}
We have translated the problem of finding the intersection of two lines or functions into finding the roots of a polynomial.
This ``problem'' conversion is very useful, because we know how to find roots of equations with algebraic methods.
Which gives us that $x=7$.

This condition, when we equate one function to another $f(x) = g(x)$, is normally referred to as the \emph{equilibrium condition}.

\subsection{Intersections of a function.}

Since a good strategy to find equilibrium conditions is to translate the equation into finding roots of polynomials, let's recall what a root of a polynomial is.
A root of a polynomial is the value or set of values of the domain that makes the function equal to zero.
That is, all the values of $x$ that satisfy the condition $f(x)=0$.

It is important to remember that a polynomial of degree $n$ has $n$ roots, assuming that the domain of the function, that is $x$, belongs to the set of complex numbers.
However, if the domain is part of the set of real numbers, this is not true for all polynomials.
For example, the function $f(x) = x^2+0.1$ does not have real roots.
This indicates that not every equation with the expression $f(x)=g(x)$ can have a solution that belongs to the set of real numbers.
And for an exam exercise, that is a valid and accurate response.

\subsection{Change of variable technique}\marginnote{Class of January 15}

In this session we will see the change of variable technique to solve an equation.
Let's recall the equation,
\begin{gather*}
    3^{3x} + 3^{x} = 3^{2x+1}.
\end{gather*}
We can push the idea of \emph{factoring} an expression, in the sense that we can algebraically modify the expression to look for similar terms.
With this in mind, let's apply the exponential properties to rewrite the equation,
\begin{gather*}
    \qty(3^{x})^3 + 3^{x} = \qty(3^{x})^2\cdot 3^1.
\end{gather*}
We can see that in all of the terms appears the factor $3^x$; hence, we can introduce a \emph{change of variable} as follows: $y=3^x$,
\begin{align*}
    y^3 + y &= y^2\cdot 3 \\
    y^3 + y - 3y^2 &= 0
\end{align*}
Now, instead of solving an exponential equation, we change the problem into finding the roots of a cubic equation.
\begin{align*}
     y\qty(y^2 - 3y^1 + 1) &= 0.
\end{align*}
Hence, one solution is $y=0$.
The other two solutions can be computed using the general formula, considering that $a=1,~b=-3,~c=1$,
\begin{align*}
    y &= \frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a} \\
      &= \frac{-(-3)\pm\sqrt{(-3)^2 -4(1)(1)}}{2(1)(1)} \\
      &= \frac{3\pm\sqrt{5}}{2}.
\end{align*}

Now that we found the three roots of the cubic polynomial, $y\in\{0,\frac{3+\sqrt{5}}{2},\frac{3-\sqrt{5}}{2}\}$, let's finish the equation by reverting the change of variable.
For the first solution we have,
\begin{align*}
    3^{x} &= y = 0 \\
    \log_{3}\qty(3^x) &= \log{3}\qty(0) \\
    x &= \log{3}\qty(0).
\end{align*}
which does not exits.
Therefore, we get only two solutions left,
\begin{align*}
    3^x &=y = \frac{3\pm\sqrt{5}}{2} \\
    \log_{3}\qty(3^x) &= \log_{3}\qty(\frac{3\pm\sqrt{5}}{2}) \\
    x &= \log_{3}\qty(\frac{3\pm\sqrt{5}}{2}).
\end{align*}
And those are the intersection points of the original equation.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Domain of \textit{all} polynomials is all real numbers, \(\mathbb{R} = (-\infty, \infty)\).

\paragraph{Rational functions} For rational functions of the form\footnote{
\textbf{Finding Domain and Range: Step-by-Step}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Domain}:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Factor numerator and denominator completely
        \item Set denominator \( Q(x) = 0 \)
        \item Domain: All real numbers except solutions to \( Q(x) = 0 \)
        \item If factors cancel (holes), note them separately
    \end{enumerate}
    
    \item \textbf{Range} (requires calculus for certainty):
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Find horizontal asymptotes: Compare degrees of \( P(x) \) and \( Q(x) \)
        \item Find vertical asymptotes: Zeros of denominator (not cancelled)
        \item Find critical points: Solve \( f'(x) = 0 \) or undefined
        \item Analyze behavior at asymptotes and critical points
        \item Consider end behavior as \( x \to \pm\infty \)
    \end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}
}:
\[
f(x) = \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}
\]
where \( P(x) \) and \( Q(x) \) are polynomials, \( Q(x) \neq 0 \).
A basic example: \( f(x) = \frac{1}{x} \)

\subsection*{Calculus Connections for Range Determination}
\marginnote{
\textbf{Important Notes for Calculus I}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Rational functions are continuous on their domains
    \item Domain restrictions affect where derivatives exist
    \item When integrating rational functions, consider partial fractions for complex denominators
    \item Always check domain before applying the Intermediate Value Theorem
    \item For optimization problems, ensure solutions are in the domain
\end{itemize}
}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Use \textbf{first derivative test} to find local extrema
    \item Use \textbf{limits at asymptotes} to determine unbounded behavior
    \[
    \lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) \quad \text{and} \quad \lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) \quad \text{for vertical asymptotes}
    \]
    \item Consider \textbf{horizontal asymptotes}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item If degree of numerator < degree of denominator: \( y = 0 \)
        \item If degrees equal: \( y = \frac{\text{leading coefficient of } P}{\text{leading coefficient of } Q} \)
        \item If degree of numerator > degree of denominator: No horizontal asymptote (may have oblique asymptote)
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Holes} occur when factors cancel: The \( y \)-value at the hole is found by evaluating the simplified function at that \( x \)-value
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{1. Addition and Subtraction of Functions}
For two functions \( f \) and \( g \), with domains \( D_f \) and \( D_g \):

\[
(f + g)(x) = f(x) + g(x), \quad (f - g)(x) = f(x) - g(x)
\]

\textbf{Domain:} The domain of \( f + g \) or \( f - g \) is \( D_f \cap D_g \).

\subsection*{2. Composition of Functions}
The composition \( f \circ g \) is defined by:

\[
(f \circ g)(x) = f(g(x))
\]

\textbf{Procedure:}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Compute \( g(x) \).
    \item Apply \( f \) to the result.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Domain:} The domain of \( f \circ g \) consists of all \( x \) in \( D_g \) such that \( g(x) \) is in \( D_f \).

\subsection*{3. Inverse Functions}
A function \( f \) has an inverse \( f^{-1} \) if it is \textbf{one-to-one} (passes the horizontal line test).

\textbf{Finding \( f^{-1} \):}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Write \( y = f(x) \).
    \item Interchange \( x \) and \( y \).
    \item Solve for \( y \).
    \item Write \( f^{-1}(x) = y \).
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Properties:}
\[
f(f^{-1}(x)) = x \quad \text{and} \quad f^{-1}(f(x)) = x
\]

\textbf{Domain and Range:}
The domain of \( f^{-1} \) is the range of \( f \), and the range of \( f^{-1} \) is the domain of \( f \).

%\[ \lim_{x \to 2} f(x) = 5 \]

Limits describe how functions behave near specific points.
The concept of \emph{Limit} it is invented as a tool to understand trends, holes and instantaneous change.
This allow to,
\begin{itemize}
    \item Define instantaneous rates of change (Derivatives)
    \item Calculate accumulated change (integrals)
    \item Analyze function behaviour near discontinuities or asymptotes
\end{itemize}
The derivative can be think of as a \emph{Instantaneous velocity}, which is the limit of your average velocity as the time interval shrinks to zero.
Without limits, we can only talk about average speed over an interval or approximate areas.

For example, let's assume that a ball is dropped from rest and its distance fallen is $d(t) = 4.9 t^2$ meters. 
Let's try to compute the speed without limits for the time $t=3$.
\begin{gather*}
    s(t) = \frac{d(t+\Delta t) - d(t)}{\Delta t},
\end{gather*}
where $\Delta$ is the time interval that we are considering.
The average speed is only an estimate. 
The ratio becomes closer to \SI{29.4}{\meter\per\second} as the time between them gets shorter\footnote{
\centering
\scriptsize
\begin{tabular}{@{}l@{\hspace{0.2em}}c@{\hspace{0.2em}}r@{}}
\toprule
Interval & \multicolumn{1}{c}{Avg. Speed} & \multicolumn{1}{c}{Result} \\
\midrule
$[3, 4]$ & $\frac{d(4)-d(3)}{1}$ & 34.3 m/s \\
$[3, 3.5]$ & $\frac{d(3.5)-d(3)}{0.5}$ & 31.85 m/s \\
$[3, 3.1]$ & $\frac{d(3.1)-d(3)}{0.1}$ & 29.89 m/s \\
$[3, 3.01]$ & $\frac{\Delta d}{0.01}$ & 29.449 m/s \\
$[3, 3.001]$ & $\frac{\Delta d}{0.001}$ & 29.4049 m/s \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\par
\vspace{0.5em}
\footnotesize
\justifying
$d(t) = 4.9t^2$ m\\
$d(3) = 44.1$ m\\
$d(4) = 78.4$ m\\
$d(3.5) = 60.025$ m\\
$d(3.1) = 47.089$ m
}
, but we can never use this method to find the exact speed at t=3. 
Why? The formula (change in d) / (change in t) needs two points and an interval. 
At the single moment $t=3$, there is no change in $t$, and dividing by zero is not possible.
We can only make better and better guesses, but we can't say what the exact amount is that they are getting closer to.

On the other hand, by using limits, we can get the exact value of the instantaneous velocity, as follows,
Starting from the previous average speed equation, however replacing the arguments of the distance function by $3+h$ and $3$, we get,
\begin{gather*}
    s(t) = \frac{d(3+h) - d(3)}{h}.
\end{gather*}
$h$ now represents the time interval, and we are going to take the limit as this time interval goes to $0$.
Next, we evaluate the distance function at the given arguments\footnote{
\begin{align*}
    s(t) &= \frac{4.9 (3+h)^2 - 4.9 (3)^2}{h} \\
         &= \frac{4.9 (9+6h+h^2) - 4.9 (9)}{h} \\
         &= \frac{44.1+29.4h+4.9h^2 - 44.1}{h} \\
         &= \frac{29.4h+4.9h^2}{h}. 
\end{align*}
},
\begin{gather*}
    s(t) = \frac{29.4h+4.9h^2}{h}.
\end{gather*}
Since $h\neq0$, because we are going to take the limit approaching $0$,
\begin{align*}
    s(t) &= 29.4+4.9h. 
\end{align*}
Now, we compute the limit as $h$ tends to zero,
\begin{align*}
    \lim_{h\to 0} s(t) &= \lim_{h\to 0}\left[29.4+4.9h\right] \\
                       &= 29.4.
\end{align*}
Hence, the instantaneous velocity at $t=3$ is \SI{29.4}{\meter\per\second}.

\subsection{Direct Substitution}

This technique mostly works for any polynomial.
For example,
\begin{align*}
    \lim_{x\to 3}\left(2x+1\right) &= 2(3)+1 \\
                                   &= 7.
\end{align*}

If you get a function that arrive into situations like $0/0$, $a/0$, we can use the L'Hopital's Rule, which if we have time we are going to cover, if not, then now you know the existance of this algebraic tool.

\subsection{Rational functions}

A common case in which you get into situations like $0/0$ and $a/0$ are the rational functions \(\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}\).
This situation emerges when the polynomials $P(x)$ and $Q(x)$ have roots in common, or at the roots of $Q(x)$.

When applying the direct substitution method gives,
\begin{align*}
    \lim_{x\to r}\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} &= \frac{P(r)}{0},
\end{align*}
then the limit is \(\pm\infty\).
To identify if it is $+\infty$ or $-\infty$, we need to check the sign of the quotient.

On the other hand, when the direct substitution method arrives to,
\begin{align*}
    \lim_{x\to r}\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} &= \frac{0}{0},
\end{align*}
then we need to make algebraic manipulation.
The main strategy is to factor and cancel common terms, and then apply the direct substitution method,
\begin{align*}
\lim_{x\to2}\frac{x^2-4}{x-2} &= \lim_{x\to2}\frac{\qty(x-2)\qty(x+2)}{x-2} \\
                              &= \lim_{x\to2}\qty(x+2) \\
                              &= 2+2 \\
                              &= 4
\end{align*}

Remember that for the example, the function it is not defined at $x=2$, however we can get the exact value of this function by using the idea of limit.

\subsection{Radicals}

In this session\footnote{January 28} we are going to tackle the limits for radical functions.
\marginnote{
    \textbf{Class Activity}
    Compute the limits of the following functions by using the analytical methods seen in this session.
    \begin{gather*}
        \lim_{x\to-1}\sqrt{-x}  \\
        \lim_{x\to1/2}\sqrt{2x+\frac{1}{2}} \\
        \lim_{x\to1}\sqrt{x-2} \\
        \lim_{x\to4}\frac{x-4}{\sqrt{x}-2}
    \end{gather*}

Recalling that a rational function has the following algebraic expression:
\begin{gather*}
    f(x) = a\sqrt{bx+h}+k.
\end{gather*}
Let's remember that the domain of this functions are such that \(bx+h\geq0\to x\geq-h/b.\)
This means that if it is asked to compute a limit that is less than \(-h/b\) the limit is not defined.
Evemore, the limit to that value only defined when approaching from $\pm\infty$, depending on the sign of $b$.
For example,
\begin{align*}
    \lim_{x\to 1} \sqrt{x-1} &= \sqrt{1-1} = \sqrt{0} = 0.
\end{align*}
This is consistent, since $x\geq 0$.
If we evaluate,
\begin{align*}
    \lim_{x\to 0.5} \sqrt{x-1} &= \sqrt{0.5-1} = \sqrt{-1/2}.
\end{align*}
In this case $x\leq 0$, therefore the limit does not exist.

As a final example, let's see the limits of \(\sqrt{x}\) from $\pm\infty$ as $x\to0$,
\begin{align*}
    \lim_{x\to 0^{-}}\sqrt{x} &= \text{DNE} \\
    \lim_{x\to 0^{+}}\sqrt{x} &= 0
\end{align*}

Another interesting function to analyse limits is when we have a rational function of radical functions, as follows
\begin{gather*}
    \lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sqrt{x+4}-2}{x}.
\end{gather*}
In order to apply the direct substitution method, we need to make algebraic modifications.
In this case we are going to use the powerfull technique of multiplying by \num{1}, made from the conjugate, that is,
\begin{gather*}
    1 = \frac{\sqrt{x+4}+2}{\sqrt{x+4}+2}.
\end{gather*}
Let's multiply by \num{1} the function\footnote{
    \begin{align*}
    \frac{\sqrt{x+4}-2}{x} &= \frac{\sqrt{x+4}-2}{x}\frac{\sqrt{x+4}+2}{\sqrt{x+4}+2} \\
                           &= \frac{\qty(x+4)-4}{x\sqrt{x+4}+2} \\
                           &= \frac{x}{x\sqrt{x+4}+2} \\
                           &=\frac{1}{\sqrt{x+4}+2}
    \end{align*}
} and we get,
\begin{align*}
    \frac{\sqrt{x+4}-2}{x} =\frac{1}{\sqrt{x+4}+2}
\end{align*}
Now, let's try the direct substitution after this algebraic manipulation,
\begin{align*}
    \lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sqrt{x+4}-2}{x} &= \lim_{x\to0}\frac{1}{\sqrt{x+4}+2} \\
                                       &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{(0)+4}+2} \\
                                       &= \frac{1}{4}
\end{align*}

\section{Limits at infinity}

In this session\footnote{January 29} we are going to see how to compute the limit of a function when the domain goes to $\pm\infty$.

\subsection{Properties of Limits}

Before delving into computing limits of rational functions, it is important to mention that we are going to need some properties of limits\footnote{
\begin{align*}
    \lim_{x\to c} b = b &\quad\text{Limit~of~a~constant} \\
    \lim_{x\to c} x = c  &\quad\text{Direct~substitution~technique} \\
    \lim_{x\to c} x^n = c^n &\quad\text{Direct~substitution}
\end{align*}

Assumming that $b$ and $c$ are real numbers, $n$ a positive integer and let $f(x)$ and $g(x)$ be functions with the following limits \(\lim_{x\to c}f(x)=L\) and \(\lim_{x\to c}f(x)=K\).
\begin{align*}
    \text{Scalar~multiple:} &\quad \lim_{x\to c}\left[b f(x)\right]=bL\\
    \text{Sum~or~Diff:} &\quad \lim_{x\to c}\left[f(x)\pm g(x)\right]=L\pm K\\
    \text{Product:} &\quad \lim_{x\to c}\left[f(x)g(x)\right]=LK\\
    \text{Quotient:} &\quad \lim_{x\to c}\left[\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}\right]=\frac{L}{K} \\
    \text{Power:} &\quad \lim_{x\to c}\left[f(x)\right]^{n}=L^{n}
\end{align*}
}.
The demostrations is a little bit outside the scope of this course, but if you are curious in off, you can ask me.

\subsection{Rational functions}

Let's review a helpful limit to examine any rational function before we begin learning how to compute these limits \(\lim_{\pm\infty}\frac{1}{x} = 0\).
This limit can be easily proved using a numerical approach.

Now, let's remember that a rational function is expressed as,
\begin{gather*}
    f(x) = \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}.
\end{gather*}
With \(P(x) = a_{n}x^{n} + a_{n-1}x^{n-1} + \ldots+a_{0}\) and \(Q(x) = b_{m}x^{m} + b_{m-1}x^{m-1} + \ldots+b_{0}\).
In order to compute the limit to $\pm\infty$, first factorize an $x$.
Then, simplify the expression algebraically and apply the previously established properties of limits.
For example to compute \(\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{x+2}{x^2+1}\) we need to re-write\footnote{
    \begin{align*}
        \frac{x+2}{x+1} &= \frac{x\qty(1+2/x)}{x\qty(x+1/x)} \\
                        &= \frac{1+2/x}{x+1/x}
    \end{align*}
} the function as 
\begin{align*}
     \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{x+2}{x^2+1}&= \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{1+2/x}{x+1/x}.
\end{align*}
Applying the properties of limits\footnote{
    \begin{align*}
        \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{1+2/x}{x+1/x} &= \frac{\lim_{x\to\infty}1+\lim_{x\to\infty}2/x}{\lim_{x\to\infty}x+\lim_{x\to\infty}1/x} \\
                                             &= \frac{1+0}{\infty+0}
    \end{align*}
},
\begin{gather*}
     \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{x+2}{x^2+1}= \frac{1}{\infty} = 0.
\end{gather*}

Another approach can be by dividing by the highest factor\footnote{
    \begin{align*}
        \frac{x+2}{x^2+1}&=\frac{x/x^2+2/x^2}{x^2/x^2+1/x^2} \\
                         &=\frac{1/x+2/x^2}{1+1/x^2}
    \end{align*}
}, in this case will be $x^2$, therefore:
\begin{align*}
     \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{x+2}{x^2+1}&= \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{1/x+2/x^2}{1+1/x^2}.
\end{align*}
Again, applying properties of limits\footnote{
\begin{align*}
    \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{1/x+2/x^2}{1+1/x^2}&= \frac{\lim_{x\to\infty}1/x + \lim_{x\to\infty}2/x^2}{\lim_{x\to\infty}1+\lim_{x\to\infty}1/x^2} \\
                                              &= \frac{0+0}{1+0} \\
                                              &= \frac{0}{1}
\end{align*}
},
\begin{gather*}
     \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{x+2}{x^2+1}= \frac{0}{1}=0.
\end{gather*}
